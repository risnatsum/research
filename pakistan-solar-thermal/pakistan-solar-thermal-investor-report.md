## 1. Solar Resource, Energy Infrastructure & Market Context

### Pakistan Solar Resource, Energy Infrastructure & Market Context

**1. Solar Resource: Among the Best in the World**

Pakistan sits within one of the planet's most irradiance-rich solar corridors. According to the World Bank's Solar Resource Mapping study and the Global Solar Atlas, the national annual mean Global Horizontal Irradiance (GHI) is approximately 2,071 kWh/m², well above the threshold for commercially viable solar projects (Source: https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/200771468096863184/pdf/95710-REVISED-ESM.pdf). Direct Normal Irradiance (DNI) — the metric most relevant to concentrating solar thermal systems — averages around 2,159 kWh/m² nationally, with peak values reaching 2,746 kWh/m² in northern Balochistan (Source: https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/200771468096863184/pdf/95710-REVISED-ESM.pdf).

The regional breakdown is decisive for site selection:

- **Balochistan** is the undisputed frontrunner. Approximately 83% of its land area exceeds 2,000 kWh/m² GHI, peak sun hours average 7–8 per day, and DNI maximums in the northwest and north make it optimal for both flat-plate and concentrating solar thermal applications. The World Bank estimated total solar potential of 1.2 million MW in the province (Source: https://energy.balochistan.gov.pk/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Solar-and-Wind-Potential.pdf).
- **Sindh** averages 6–7 peak sun hours per day with GHI comfortably above 2,000 kWh/m² across most of the province. Southern Sindh, including areas around Hyderabad and Tharparkar, records among the highest GHI values in the country (Source: https://ecosparksolar.com/peak-sun-hours-in-pakistan/).
- **Punjab** receives 400–440 cal/cm²/day — solid performance supporting robust flat-plate solar thermal applications, particularly relevant given Punjab is Pakistan's largest industrial base (Source: https://www.paspk.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/LS-931.pdf).
- **KPK** has the weakest resource profile among the four major provinces, with lower insolation and higher cloud cover in mountainous sub-regions. Least compelling for large-scale solar thermal deployment.

**2. The Energy Crisis: Acute and Structurally Entrenched**

Pakistan's energy situation as of 2024–2025 is not a cyclical disruption — it is a compounding structural crisis with severe cost consequences for industrial and commercial users.

On the electricity side: tariffs rose 155% between 2021 and 2024, driven by IMF-mandated subsidy removal and the burden of capacity payments to Independent Power Producers (IPPs), which reached Rs 2.1 trillion in projected FY2025 payments — equivalent to Rs 17.31/kWh just in fixed capacity charges (Source: https://issi.org.pk/issue-brief-on-pakistans-energy-crisis-challenges-and-path-forward/). Industrial consumers were paying approximately Rs 30–34/kWh by mid-2024. Load-shedding for industrial zones averaged 8–16 hours per day in 2024. Circular debt in the power sector stood at Rs 2,310 billion as of May 2024 (Source: https://issi.org.pk/issue-brief-on-pakistans-energy-crisis-challenges-and-path-forward/).

On the gas side: SNGPL's prescribed gas price reached Rs 1,673.82/MMBTU effective January 2024 (Source: https://www.dawn.com/news/1868600). Gas load-shedding was formally implemented October 2023 through March 2024, affecting industrial and commercial consumers during peak winter months. LNG import constraints compounded domestic gas field depletion.

**3. Industrial Heat Demand: Large and Underserved**

Industry accounts for over 37% of Pakistan's total primary energy consumption — more than 14 million tonnes of oil equivalent (MTOE) in FY2023 (Source: https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099071425022531718/pdf/P507230-2df500e8-b745-44f8-b3ec-0be5216ba86a.pdf). The industrial sector also consumes 73% of Pakistan's coal, much of it burned for process heat (Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11216625/).

The World Bank identified five priority sectors: textiles, cement, steel, fertiliser, and paper/pulp. Textiles are the most consequential — contributing nearly one-quarter of industrial value added and employing ~40% of the industrial workforce. Within textiles, dyeing and finishing consume heat at temperatures in the 60–160°C range — directly addressable by solar thermal.

**4. Solar Thermal Adoption: Early Stage, Data-Sparse**

Pakistan's solar thermal sector is nascent with poor formal data coverage. No major international statistical body — IRENA, REN21, or IEA-SHC — publishes a discrete Pakistan MWth figure; the country appears in "rest of world" or is absent entirely (Source: https://www.iea-shc.org/solar-heat-worldwide). Growth since 2015 is reported anecdotally as strong, but no reliable quantitative baseline exists [UNVERIFIED].

What is clear is that broader solar adoption has been explosive: Pakistan imported a record 16.6 GW of solar PV panels in 2024 alone, and cumulative net-metered rooftop PV hit 5.3 GW by April 2025 — a tenfold increase in two years (Source: https://www.ren21.net/gsr-2025/snapshots/pk/). This normalises solar as a technology choice among commercial and industrial buyers.

**5. Macro Tailwinds: 2025–2030 Is a Structural Inflection Window**

Pakistan's GDP grew 3.0% in FY2025, with the World Bank projecting 3.1–3.4% growth in FY2026–27 (Source: https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2025/04/23/pakistan-structural-reforms-needed-to-turn-economic-stabilization-into-sustained-inclusive-growth-says-world-bank). Recovering industrial output creates heat demand just as energy costs remain at historically high levels. Pakistan's urban population is growing at ~2.4% annually — South Asia's fastest urbanisation rate (Source: https://tradingeconomics.com/pakistan/urban-population-growth-annual-percent-wb-data.html). Total primary energy demand is projected to reach 99.2 MTOE by 2030 (Source: https://www.pc.gov.pk/uploads/report/IEP_Report_FINAL.pdf). The circular debt trap means grid electricity cost relief is structurally unlikely.

**Key Findings:**
- Pakistan's solar resource is world-class: GHI of 2,071 kWh/m² nationally, DNI peaking at 2,746 kWh/m² in Balochistan; Balochistan and Sindh are optimal for solar thermal, Punjab is the priority commercial market due to industrial density.
- The energy crisis is acute and structurally unlikely to self-resolve: industrial electricity tariffs rose 155% from 2021–2024, gas load-shedding ran through full winter seasons, and Rs 2.3 trillion circular debt creates a locked-in cost environment making on-site heat generation economically compelling.
- Industry consumes over 37% of Pakistan's primary energy (14+ MTOE/year), with textiles, cement, fertiliser, and steel as the dominant heat consumers — dyeing/finishing processes operate at temperatures squarely within flat-plate and ETC solar thermal range.
- Solar thermal adoption data is essentially unmeasured at the national level — neither IRENA, IEA-SHC, nor AEDB publish a Pakistan-specific MWth figure.
- The 2025–2030 window is structurally distinct: energy price pain at historic peak, solar supply chains mature and cost-optimised, economic recovery generating industrial capex, urbanisation accelerating commercial demand.

**Could Not Verify:**
- Precise share of Pakistan's total energy consumption attributable specifically to industrial process heat (disaggregated from mechanical, electrical, transport).
- Pakistan's total installed solar thermal capacity in MWth and number of installations.
- Growth trajectory of solar thermal since 2015 in quantitative terms.
- Specific RLNG prices paid by industrial off-takers vs. pipeline gas tariffs.

## 2. Industrial Process Heat — Textile & Leather

### Industrial Process Heat — Textile & Leather Sector

Pakistan's textile industry is one of the largest in Asia and is structurally well-suited for solar thermal displacement of low-to-medium temperature process heat. The combination of high energy prices, gas rationing, and strong solar irradiation creates a rare alignment of investor conditions — though the sector's financial distress means deployment capital must come from outside the mills themselves.

**Scale and Structure of the Textile Industry**

Pakistan ranks third in Asia by installed spinning capacity, with approximately 13.4 million spindles and 250+ active spinning mills producing roughly 2.5 million metric tonnes of yarn in FY2024 (Source: https://docs.vis.com.pk/docs/TextileSectorReport.pdf). The broader textile chain includes 1,221 ginning units, 442 spinning units, and hundreds of dyeing, printing, and finishing (DP&F) processing facilities. The sector accounts for 52% of Pakistan's total export earnings and roughly 8.5% of GDP, employing an estimated 30–40% of the industrial workforce (Source: https://docs.vis.com.pk/docs/TextileSectorReport.pdf). Geographic concentration is highly pronounced: Faisalabad (Punjab) hosts the largest cluster of spinning and weaving mills; Karachi dominates denim and SITE area processing; Sialkot and Lahore are secondary hubs (Source: https://www.v-trust.com/en/blog/pakistan-suppliers-a-buyers-guide-on-manufacturing-clusters).

Of the sub-sectors, dyeing and finishing is the most energy-intensive, with beamhouse-equivalent wet processing requiring sustained heat input across bleaching, mercerising, dyeing baths, steaming, and drying stages (Source: https://www.energystar.gov/sites/default/files/buildings/tools/EE_Guidebook_for_Textile_industry.pdf).

**Process Heat Requirements**

Approximately 70–80% of total energy consumption in textile processing is thermal energy; the remainder is electrical (Source: https://www.globalefficiencyintel.com/electrification-of-heating-in-the-textile-industry). Key process temperatures: mercerisation at 60–70°C; reactive dyeing baths at 60–90°C; continuous bleaching above 100°C; high-pressure dyeing of synthetics at 130–150°C. More than 74% of Pakistan's total industrial energy consumption goes to process heat demand (Source: https://www.paspk.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/LS-931.pdf). Critically, the bulk of textile process heat falls below 150°C — the practical ceiling for non-concentrating solar thermal collectors, and well within what FPC and ETC systems deliver without concentration.

**Energy Cost Burden on the Textile Sector**

Pakistan's textile sector is in acute energy-cost distress. APTMA has documented that energy now accounts for 35–55% of conversion costs in upstream segments — compared to under 20% in competing economies like India, Bangladesh, and Vietnam (Source: https://aptma.org.pk/export-sector-prohibitive-energy-costs/). Industrial electricity rates reached approximately Rs 38–40 per kWh (roughly 14–15 US cents/kWh) by early 2025 — more than double the 9 US cents/kWh the industry considers the competitive threshold (Source: https://propakistani.pk/2024/02/14/increasing-energy-prices-hurting-pakistans-textile-and-apparel-exports-aptma/). As of early 2025, at least 187 textile mills had halted operations (Source: https://tribune.com.pk/story/2528200/187-mills-shut-down-in-punjab). This cost crisis is the single strongest demand-side driver for solar thermal: mills that remain operating are actively seeking to displace boiler fuel at any viable payback.

**Leather Industry Profile**

Pakistan's leather industry comprises an estimated 800 tanneries, with 364 companies registered with the SECP, and directly employs approximately 200,000–500,000 workers (Source: https://blackbirdleathers.com/leather-industry-in-pakistan/). Kasur (Punjab) alone holds 275 tanneries, making it the dominant processing cluster (Source: https://www.pakistantanners.org/). Industry exports reached approximately USD 1.0 billion in FY2022-23 (Source: https://tdap.gov.pk/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/Finished-Leather-Report_.pdf).

Leather processing is thermally intensive: beamhouse operations at 30–45°C; post-tanning dyeing and fat-liquoring at 50–70°C; drying and setting at 70–90°C. One tannery energy study found 72% of total energy from natural gas, 27% from electricity (Source: https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/15/6/5599). The temperature profile falls almost entirely below 100°C — arguably a cleaner solar thermal fit than textiles.

**Solar Thermal Installations — Current State and Case Studies**

Documented solar thermal deployments in Pakistan's textile or leather sectors are extremely sparse. The most substantive published work is a peer-reviewed study modelling FPC and ETC collectors for boiler feedwater preheating in Pakistani textile mills across five locations, finding annual solar fractions of 0.22–0.32 without storage and 0.20–0.60 with thermal storage, with the highest NPV achieved in Quetta against coal displacement (NPV of approximately PKR 1,509 million per modelled system) (Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/article/abs/pii/S1755008422000461).

No named commercial solar thermal installations in a Pakistani textile or leather facility could be confirmed through this research. The solar activity currently visible in the textile sector is entirely solar PV: mills in Faisalabad are executing 10–15-year PPAs for rooftop PV at under PKR 15/unit, with an estimated 15–20% of Faisalabad mills having partially shifted to solar electricity (Source: https://loksujag.com/story/textile-industry-solar-energy-eng). This represents an important competitive dynamic: PV adoption is moving faster than solar thermal, and any solar thermal investment pitch must account for the fact that mills are already committing roof space and capital to PV.

**Addressable Market**

Research confirms that cotton-based textile clusters in Pakistan can achieve solar fractions of 22–60% depending on storage configuration. With approximately 250 active spinning mills and several hundred DP&F processing units remaining operational, plus 800 tanneries in leather — even at a conservative 25% solar fraction applied to the low-temperature heat load of the remaining ~370 operating textile factories and ~500 active tanneries, the addressable thermal displacement runs into hundreds of MWth of annual demand [UNVERIFIED — no Pakistan-specific national solar thermal industrial heat potential study was located].

**Key Findings:**
- Energy cost in Pakistan's textile sector has reached 35–55% of upstream conversion costs — more than double the competitive benchmark — making solar thermal's value proposition commercially compelling for any mill that can access the capital.
- The dominant process heat demand in both textiles (dyeing, finishing, sizing) and leather (beamhouse, fat-liquoring, drying) falls below 150°C, directly within the delivery range of standard non-concentrating solar thermal collectors (FPC and ETC).
- No confirmed commercial-scale solar thermal installation in a Pakistani textile or leather facility was found; current solar investment in the sector is almost entirely PV-focused, creating a greenfield opportunity but also a sequencing risk — roof space and capital are already being allocated to PV.
- The leather sector, concentrated in Kasur (275 tanneries) and overwhelmingly gas/steam-dependent, with process temperatures below 100°C throughout, represents a particularly clean solar thermal fit and a tightly clustered geographic target.

**Could Not Verify:**
- A Pakistan-specific national solar thermal industrial process heat potential estimate in MWth or PJ/year.
- The exact current count of operational dyeing and finishing units in Pakistan.
- Any named, documented solar thermal installation (as distinct from PV) in a Pakistani textile or leather factory.
- Specific gas pricing and energy cost data for leather tanneries.

## 3. Food & Beverage — Solar Thermal Applications

### Food & Beverage Industry — Solar Thermal Applications

**Pakistan's F&B Sector: Scale and Structure**

Pakistan's food and beverage processing industry is the country's second-largest manufacturing sector after textiles, accounting for approximately 27% of manufacturing value-added and 16% of manufacturing employment (Source: https://tdap.gov.pk/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/processed-foodd.pdf). The Census of Manufacturing Industries (2015–16) recorded approximately 924 registered food and beverage manufacturing units; industry sources cite 2,500+ total food processing units including smaller operations (Source: https://www.pbs.gov.pk/industry-2/).

Key subsectors include: **dairy** (three major formal-sector processors — Nestle Pakistan at ~1,168 million litres/year, Engro Foods at ~584 million litres/year, Nurpur at ~237 million litres/year — though formal processors collectively handle only ~20% of Pakistan's total milk supply) (Source: https://grain.org/en/article/6738-the-corporate-attack-on-pakistan-s-small-dairies); **beverages** (~170 operating units); **edible oil** (~150 processing plants producing 2.71 million tonnes/year); **flour milling** (1,200+ registered mills with 10.8 million tonnes/year capacity as of FY2024); and **sugar** (with cogeneration from bagasse increasingly common).

**Process Heat Requirements by Subsector**

Heat — not electricity — dominates energy use in F&B manufacturing globally, with roughly two-thirds of plant energy going to process heating (Source: https://www.energy.gov/cmei/ito/food-and-beverage-products). Temperature requirements by subsector:

- **Dairy:** Pasteurisation at 72–75°C (HTST); UHT processing at 135°C; hot water CIP cleaning at 80–90°C. A research study on solar-assisted yogurt processing in Pakistan used an ETC collector to heat milk to 80°C, with heat transfer efficiency above 80% (Source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/energy-research/articles/10.3389/fenrg.2022.887639/full).
- **Beverages:** Bottle washing, pasteurisation, and syrup preparation require 60–100°C. A feasibility study modelled solar thermal at 180°C steam for beverage plants, finding 17–23% solar share at 1.5–2.0 MWth scale (Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0360544221014018).
- **Edible oil:** Hydrogenation and deodorisation require 150–200°C; degumming and bleaching at 60–100°C — lower-temperature stages compatible with non-concentrating solar thermal.
- **Flour milling:** Primarily mechanical/electrical — weak candidate for solar thermal.
- **Sugar:** Evaporation at 100–120°C, but most Pakistani mills self-generate heat by burning bagasse, reducing the commercial case.

**Energy Cost Burden and Price Trajectory**

Industrial gas for captive power has risen from Rs 1,087/MMBTU (October 2020) to Rs 3,500/MMBTU (February 2025) — a 3.2× increase in nominal terms (Source: https://augaf.com/pakistan-increases-gas-prices-for-captive-power-plants-to-12-6-mmbtu/). Industrial electricity hit $132/MWh in 2024, up ~47% in a single year (Source: https://www.globalpetrolprices.com/Pakistan/electricity_prices/). This rapid and sustained cost inflation materially strengthens the investment case for fuel-displacement technologies including solar thermal.

**Documented Installations and Case Studies**

No commissioned, publicly documented solar thermal installation for commercial food or beverage processing in Pakistan could be identified. The closest Pakistan-specific evidence is academic: a proof-of-concept solar-assisted yogurt processing unit using a 2.5 m² evacuated tube collector, demonstrating technical viability at artisanal/farm scale but not commercial scale (Source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/energy-research/articles/10.3389/fenrg.2022.887639/full).

The most directly comparable regional evidence comes from **India**. Under a UNDP-GEF project supported by MNRE, the National Dairy Development Board (NDDB) completed 15 concentrating solar thermal (CST) installations in dairy cooperative processing plants, reducing annual heat demand by 5–15% (Source: https://www.nddb.coop/node/1778). A ScienceDirect study on financial viability of SHIP in Indian dairy found that without government Viability Gap Funding, payback periods were too long to attract private investment unaided, but with support could compress to ~3 years (Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2213138817302412).

**Technical and Commercial Barriers**

IRENA identifies the core barriers as: (1) high upfront capital cost relative to short payback expectations; (2) space constraints — large collector aperture areas conflict with dense factory footprints; (3) process integration complexity — matching intermittent solar supply to continuous thermal demand requires buffer storage; and (4) risk aversion from plant engineers and procurement managers (Source: https://www.irena.org/-/media/Files/IRENA/Agency/Publication/2015/IRENA_ETSAP_Tech_Brief_E21_Solar_Heat_Industrial_2015.pdf). In Pakistan specifically: weak project finance, limited local engineering capacity, and — until recently — artificially suppressed gas prices.

**Addressable Market Estimate**

Applying the food sector's 27% manufacturing share against Pakistan's ~21.54 Mtoe total industrial energy consumption (Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11216625/), and assuming ~60–65% of food sector energy is thermal (consistent with global benchmarks), implies roughly 3.5–4 Mtoe of thermal energy in the food sector annually. The fraction addressable by low-to-medium temperature solar thermal (below 200°C) — covering dairy, beverage CIP, and low-temperature edible oil stages — is conservatively 30–40% of that thermal load, or approximately 1–1.6 Mtoe/year. [UNVERIFIED — no official MWth market sizing study for Pakistan's F&B solar thermal market exists in public domain]

**Key Findings:**
- Pakistan's F&B sector is the country's second-largest industry (27% of manufacturing value-added), with dairy, beverages, and edible oil all having significant low-to-medium temperature heat demand compatible with solar thermal below 200°C.
- Industrial energy costs have surged dramatically: captive gas prices rose 3.2× from 2020 to 2025; industrial electricity hit $132/MWh in 2024 — fundamentally altering the solar thermal investment case.
- No commissioned commercial solar thermal installation in Pakistan's F&B sector is documented in public sources; the closest comparator is India's NDDB programme (15 CST installations in dairy cooperatives, 5–15% heat demand reduction), which required government Viability Gap Funding to reach viable payback periods.
- The primary barriers are upfront capital cost, factory space constraints, process integration complexity, and historically suppressed gas prices — the last of which is rapidly being removed.

**Could Not Verify:**
- Specific annual energy consumption data for Pakistan's food and beverage manufacturing sector.
- Any AEDB or PPIB formal programme for solar thermal industrial process heat in F&B.
- A formal addressable market size (MWth or USD) for solar thermal in Pakistan's F&B sector.

## 4. Hospitality & Residential Solar Thermal Market

### Hospitality & Residential Solar Thermal Market

**Sector Scale and the Hot Water Problem**

Pakistan's hospitality and institutional landscape presents a substantial addressable market for solar thermal. The country has approximately 1,201 registered hospitals (government and private combined) (Source: https://www.emro.who.int/pak/programmes/service-delivery.html). Higher education comprises 262 HEC-recognised universities and degree-awarding institutions as of 2024 (Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_Education_Commission_(Pakistan)). The hotel market was valued at USD 1.49 billion in 2024 (Source: https://www.statista.com/outlook/mmo/travel-tourism/hotels/pakistan).

The energy cost pressure on these operators is acute. Pakistan's government raised gas tariffs by up to 193% in November 2023 under IMF fiscal reform conditions, followed by a further 35% increase in February 2024 (Source: https://www.enerdata.net/publications/daily-energy-news/pakistans-government-raises-gas-tariffs-172.html). Hotels, bakeries, and small commercial operators were specifically called out as badly affected. Globally, hot water and space heating account for 6–8% of a hotel's total operating costs (Source: https://aemaco.com/2025/01/16/10-must-know-hotel-energy-consumption-statistics/).

**Commercial Adoption: What Has Actually Happened**

Documented commercial solar thermal adoption in Pakistan remains sparse in publicly available data. The most concrete example is a 52-room hotel powered by a solar thermal system — cited in a commercial-sector case study but without a named property or city (Source: https://www.builditsolar.com/Projects/WaterHeating/PakistanSHW/PakistanSHW.htm). A peer-reviewed study on student hostel buildings in Pakistan assessed the techno-economic case for solar water heater refurbishment, finding it viable at current tariff levels (Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2666278724000138). UNICEF reported in May 2024 that energy-resilient installations across Pakistan's health facilities could add USD 296 million in economic value, implying hospital solar adoption remains at pilot stage (Source: https://propakistani.pk/2024/05/31/energy-resilience-in-health-facilities-to-save-lives-and-add-296-million-to-pakistans-economy-unicef/).

Pakistan's leading solar water heater supplier Eimex Solar claims over 7,000 cumulative installations since 2014, across residential and commercial segments combined (Source: https://eimexco.com/about/). This is the most concrete market-scale data point available and suggests the overall installed base across all segments is in the low tens of thousands of units at most.

**Residential Market**

The residential solar water heater (SWH) market had estimated demand of PKR 4.1 billion (~USD 14–15 million at 2024 exchange rates) by 2024 (Source: https://www.6wresearch.com/industry-report/pakistan-solar-water-heater-market-2020-2026). The dominant technology is flat plate collectors, though evacuated tube collectors (ETCs) are gaining share globally (44.2% global revenue share in 2023) (Source: https://www.gminsights.com/industry-analysis/solar-water-heater-market).

Retail price points for residential systems range from PKR 40,000 to PKR 115,000 for 100–150-litre systems and PKR 130,000–360,000+ for larger or premium units (Source: https://thesolarpanelpriceinpakistan.pk/solar-water-heater-price-in-pakistan-2025/). A peer-reviewed cost-benefit study of Islamabad residential consumers found a payback period of 1.16–1.38 years versus electric heating and 6.95–8.27 years versus gas — the electric-replacement case is compelling; the gas-replacement case was marginal before the 2023–24 tariff shock but has likely improved materially since (Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959652617328391).

**Leading Suppliers**

Eimex Solar (est. 1998, solar water heating since 2014) is the market leader by self-reported installation count — 7,000+ installations, ISO and PS certified, with nationwide distribution and 10-year warranties (Source: https://eimexco.com/about/). Alpha Solar and Raj Solar (Karachi-based) are active competitors. Haier units from PKR 35,600 and Gree from PKR 30,600 compete on brand trust and retail availability (Source: https://thesolarpanelpriceinpakistan.pk/solar-water-heater-price-in-pakistan-2025/).

**Barriers to Faster Adoption**

Five structural barriers constrain the market: (1) high upfront cost — a residential system at PKR 100,000–120,000 represents two to three months of median urban household income; (2) gas subsidy legacy — despite 2023–24 tariff hikes, residential gas prices for protected consumers remain subsidised; (3) limited financing — bank green lending products not widely accessible for households without formal credit history (Source: https://www.adb.org/sites/default/files/publication/460346/adbi-wp880.pdf); (4) awareness gaps; and (5) product quality concerns — government-installed systems have shown leakage and noise issues, undermining trust (Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959652617328391).

**Growth Trajectory 2025–2030**

The global SWH market is growing at ~5.5% CAGR through 2030 (Source: https://www.gminsights.com/industry-analysis/solar-water-heater-market). For Pakistan to achieve 3–5x growth by 2030, the following conditions would need to hold: (1) gas prices remain elevated; (2) financing products become accessible; (3) quality standards and installer certification improve; and (4) hotel and hospital procurement decision-makers are reached through industry association channels. The IMF-driven gas pricing reform path creates a structural tailwind.

**Key Findings:**
- Gas tariff hikes of up to 193% in late 2023 dramatically improved the economics of solar water heating for Pakistan's hotels, hospitals, and residential users — payback period versus electric heating is already under 1.5 years per peer-reviewed Islamabad research.
- The commercial adoption base is very thin — the market leader Eimex Solar reports ~7,000 cumulative installations across all segments combined, suggesting the sector is at early commercialisation rather than scale.
- Pakistan's residential SWH market was estimated at PKR 4.1 billion (~USD 14–15 million) by 2024, with demand driven by rising tariffs and growing awareness — but specific installed-base unit counts are not available in public data.
- The primary barriers are upfront cost and financing access, not technology viability — the gap is in accessible credit products and installer quality assurance.

## 5. Policy, Incentives & Financing Landscape

### Policy, Incentives & Financing Landscape for Solar Thermal in Pakistan

**The Critical Distinction: Solar Thermal vs. Solar PV**

Nearly every Pakistani government policy, incentive, and financing program that invokes "solar energy" is designed for photovoltaic (PV) systems generating electricity — not for solar thermal systems generating heat. An investor must read every incentive through this lens before assuming applicability.

**Government Policy and Regulatory Framework**

Pakistan's primary renewable energy regulator, AEDB, was merged into the Private Power and Infrastructure Board (PPIB) in June 2023 (Source: https://hetco.pk/ppib-aedb-registration-2024-solar-imports-installations/). Neither AEDB's historical mandate nor the post-merger PPIB structure contains a dedicated solar thermal track. AEDB's involvement in solar thermal has historically been limited to small-scale dissemination: in 2013, the board facilitated the import of approximately 14,981 solar water heaters and supported a limited awareness campaign — no comparable program has been reported since (Source: https://apctt.org/sites/default/files/2020-08/Country_Report_pakistan.pdf).

The Pakistan Council of Renewable Energy Technologies (PCRET), under the Ministry of Science and Technology, formally includes solar thermal in its mandate but operates primarily as a research and dissemination body with no significant capital deployment role (Source: http://www.pcret.gov.pk/). NEPRA has no regulatory jurisdiction over non-electric solar thermal systems. Pakistan's Alternative and Renewable Energy Policy (2019) set a 30% renewable electricity target by 2030 but contains no specific solar thermal targets (Source: https://power.gov.pk/Policies).

**Tax Incentives**

The FBR explicitly confirmed in December 2023 that the sales tax exemption under the Sixth Schedule covers only "photovoltaic cells whether or not assembled in modules or made up into panels" — solar thermal collectors, flat-plate collectors, evacuated tubes, and solar water heaters are not mentioned and are therefore subject to the standard 18% GST and applicable customs duties (Source: https://www.energyupdate.com.pk/2023/12/02/only-photovoltaic-cells-exempted-from-sales-tax-fbr/). Budget 2024–25 changes applied to PV panels only; solar thermal equipment was not addressed (Source: https://www.pvknowhow.com/news/tax-incentives-for-solar-panels-in-pakistan/). [UNVERIFIED: Whether solar thermal equipment qualifies under any separate machinery import SRO as "plant and machinery" eligible for reduced duty]

**Concessional Financing**

The State Bank of Pakistan (SBP) operates a Financing Scheme for Renewable Energy with a markup rate capped at 6% per annum, maximum tenor of 12 years including a 2-year grace period for large projects, and a per-borrower limit of Rs. 6 billion for large projects (Source: https://www.sbp.org.pk/Incen-others/Rene.asp). As of June 2024, Rs. 94.7 billion had been cumulatively disbursed (Source: https://www.sbp.org.pk/about/speech/Governors/JameelAhmad/2024/08-Nov-2024.pdf). The scheme text does not explicitly name eligible technologies — meaning solar thermal may technically qualify, but in practice the entire scheme infrastructure is built around solar PV. [UNVERIFIED: Whether any bank has actually disbursed SBP refinance funds for a solar thermal project]

No IFC, IsDB, USAID, or World Bank program specifically targeting solar thermal industrial process heat in Pakistan was identified. GIZ's Pakistan energy transition project and ADB's Access to Clean Energy Investment Program are both PV-only.

**Regulatory and Political Risk**

The most significant regulatory risk signal was the government's forced renegotiation and termination of five IPP Power Purchase Agreements in 2024 — with HUBCO, Lalpir, Saba Power, Rousch Power, and Atlas Power — under pressure from a PKR 2.1 trillion capacity payments crisis (Source: https://thediplomat.com/2024/10/pakistan-renegotiates-costly-contracts-with-5-private-power-producers/). IPP representatives called this the "fourth instance of contract interference." While solar thermal off-grid systems are structurally insulated from grid offtake risk, this demonstrates a sovereign willingness to override commercial contract terms under fiscal pressure.

Currency risk has partially stabilised: the PKR traded in a range of PKR 278–282 to USD from January 2024 through April 2025. However, the US State Department notes that "U.S. companies have faced delays in profit repatriation due to unclear policies" (Source: https://www.state.gov/reports/2024-investment-climate-statements/pakistan). A January 2024 600 MW solar auction in Muzaffargarh attracted zero bids, with developers citing Pakistan's high-risk profile (Source: https://ieefa.org/resources/pakistan-must-rebuild-chinese-investor-confidence-its-energy-transition).

**The Gas Subsidy Interaction**

This is the most economically material policy variable for solar thermal's business case. Under IMF conditions, OGRA raised gas prices for captive power plants from Rs. 2,750/MMBTU (February 2024) to Rs. 3,000/MMBTU (July 2024) then Rs. 3,500/MMBTU (February 2025), with a stated target of reaching imported LNG price parity (approximately USD 10–13/MMBTU) (Source: https://augaf.com/pakistan-increases-gas-prices-for-captive-power-plants-to-12-6-mmbtu/). This structural shift materially improves solar thermal payback periods for the first time making solar process heat economically competitive with grid-supplied gas for many mid-temperature industrial applications. The risk is a policy reversal if the IMF program lapses.

**Key Findings:**
- Solar thermal receives no dedicated policy, regulatory, or tax framework in Pakistan — every named program, exemption, and financing scheme in the solar space is designed for PV. An investor cannot assume any existing solar incentive applies without explicit legal confirmation.
- Gas subsidy removal under IMF conditionality is the single most important recent development improving solar thermal's business case: industrial gas for captive power has risen to Rs. 3,500/MMBTU and is targeted at LNG import parity.
- The SBP Renewable Energy Financing Scheme at 6% p.a. / 12-year tenor is the most credible concessional finance pathway for solar thermal, but it has never been used for a solar thermal project in practice.
- Pakistan's sovereign contract risk is severe and documented: the 2024 forced renegotiation of five IPP agreements demonstrates the government's willingness to override commercial terms under fiscal pressure.

**Could Not Verify:**
- Whether any Pakistani commercial or development bank has actually disbursed the SBP renewable energy refinance facility for a solar thermal (non-PV) project.
- Whether solar thermal equipment qualifies under any FBR SRO for reduced customs duty rates distinct from the PV-only Sixth Schedule exemption.
- Current AEDB/PPIB solar water heater dissemination programs post-2020.

## 6. Competitive Landscape, Supply Chain & Technology

### Competitive Landscape, Supply Chain & Technology Profile — Pakistan Solar Thermal

**Local Manufacturers**

Pakistan's domestic solar thermal manufacturing base is thin but expanding. The oldest and most established player is **Eimex Solar** (Islamabad, group founded 1998, solar water heater division active since 2014), which claims over 7,000 installations nationwide, offering ISO and PS-certified stainless steel systems in 150–300 litre capacities (Source: https://eimexco.com/about/).

**Raj Solar Private Limited** (Karachi, founded 2022) is a newer entrant, self-described as the first true manufacturer of solar water heaters in Pakistan. It targets commercial and industrial applications with its Shamsi 200 product line and has completed installations in textile, FMCG, pharma, and hospitality — including a 52-room hotel (Source: https://rajsolar.pk/about-us/). **Zorays Solar** (Lahore, registered 2014) is a solar PV-dominant EPC firm that has expanded into solar thermal including solar hot water, solar dryers, and biogas-thermal hybrid projects, with over 300 installations of all types (Source: https://zorays.com/solar-heaters/). **Alpha Solar** and **Paksolar Services** operate as retailers/installers rather than manufacturers. Home appliance brands **Nasgas** and **Homage** have entered with white-label solar geyser products.

**Importers, Distributors, and Import Origins**

Pakistan is overwhelmingly import-dependent for solar thermal hardware. China is the dominant origin country for all solar thermal equipment, driven by price advantage, CPEC supply chain relationships, and existing freight corridors. No reliable Pakistan-specific import volume data broken down for solar thermal (as distinct from solar PV panels) was publicly accessible. For context on the broader solar import scale, Pakistan imported approximately 16.6 GW of solar PV modules from China in 2024, making it the world's third-largest destination for Chinese solar exports (Source: https://taiyangnews.info/markets/pakistan-9m-fy2025-solar-panel-imports-hit-127-gw). The Pakistan solar water heater market was estimated at PKR 4.1 billion (~USD 14.7 million) by 2024 (Source: https://www.6wresearch.com/industry-report/pakistan-solar-water-heater-market-2020-2026) [UNVERIFIED — paywalled secondary source].

Import data identifies **Ahmad Sardar Jahan Ltd** (840 shipments), **Bright Star Business Solution** (686 shipments), and **TK Enterprises SMC** (399 shipments) as top solar equipment importers from China by shipment count — though this covers all solar equipment, not thermal specifically (Source: https://www.volza.com/p/solar-panel/buyers/buyers-in-pakistan/).

**EPC Contractors and System Integrators**

For commercial and industrial solar thermal, the competitive landscape overlaps heavily with the PV EPC sector. **Reon Energy Limited** (incorporated 2014) is Pakistan's most prominent large-scale renewable EPC, with over 250 MW of solar PV deployed across industrial clients including Bestway Cement (57 MW across four plants), Unilever, Kohinoor Textile, and Sindh Engro Coal Mining (Source: https://reonenergy.com/our-history/). **Premier Energy** (Lahore, operating since 2014) targets textile and industrial clients (Source: https://premierenergy.com.pk/projects/). For dedicated solar thermal-only integration, Raj Solar and Eimex Solar represent the identifiable specialist players; larger thermal process heat projects have no named specialist EPC contractor evident in public sources.

**Technology Profile and Price Points**

Evacuated tube collectors (ETCs) dominate in the Pakistan residential and small-commercial market, consistent with Chinese manufacturing scale and global trends. Flat plate collectors (FPCs) retain a presence particularly in industrial process heat. No concentrating solar thermal (parabolic trough, linear Fresnel) has been commercially deployed in Pakistan as of 2025; academic studies have modelled viability for Quetta and Hyderabad, but no commercial CSP plant exists (Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959652621003450).

Pricing: complete residential ETC systems (150–200 litre) sell in the range of PKR 45,000–120,000; larger systems (300 litre+) exceed PKR 360,000 (Source: https://thesolarpanelpriceinpakistan.pk/solar-water-heater-price-in-pakistan-2025/). Using a standard 2 m² collector area for a 150-litre system, this implies approximately PKR 22,500–60,000/m² (roughly USD 80–215/m² at ~280 PKR/USD), consistent with global ETC pricing.

**Supply Chain Vulnerabilities and Cost Drivers**

Four structural risks stand out:

1. **Currency exposure:** The PKR depreciated 28% in FY2023. Since all solar thermal equipment is priced in USD at origin, importers carry full currency translation risk (Source: https://www.dawn.com/news/1762243).
2. **Import duty ambiguity:** Solar thermal equipment sits in a grey zone between standard machinery rates and solar-specific exemptions. Domestic water heater collectors face standard rates (CD 3–11%, plus 17% GST and ~5.5–6% advance income tax) unless covered by an SRO exemption (Source: https://tencoconsulting.com/solar-panel-import-pakistan/).
3. **Quality assurance:** The Pakistan Solar Association flagged that Pakistan risks becoming a dumping ground for substandard Chinese products (Source: https://arynews.tv/counterfeit-solar-panels-put-pakistans-solar-ambitions-in-jeopardy). This dynamic is likely to extend to solar thermal equipment.
4. **Local content unavailability:** No Pakistan-based manufacturer of core solar thermal components (evacuated tubes, selective-surface absorber plates, heat pipes) has been identified. All critical inputs are imported.

**International Players**

No major European solar thermal manufacturer (Viessmann, Bosch Thermotechnology, Vaillant, Ariston) has an identified presence or stated Pakistan market strategy. The market has been ceded entirely to Chinese manufacturers through intermediary importers. No international solar thermal-specific company has been identified targeting Pakistan as a declared growth market.

**Key Findings:**
- Domestic manufacturing is nascent — Eimex Solar (2014) and Raj Solar (2022) are the only identifiable entities with genuine manufacturing or assembly operations; the rest of the supply chain is import-driven, almost entirely from China.
- Evacuated tube collectors dominate the residential and small-commercial market by volume; flat plate collectors appear in industrial process heat; concentrating solar thermal has zero commercial deployment in Pakistan.
- The supply chain carries compounding cost risks: PKR depreciation, uncertain duty classification, and a growing quality-assurance problem as Chinese manufacturers dump B-grade inventory.
- No major European or Chinese solar thermal manufacturer has a direct Pakistan presence — the market is served through third-party importers, leaving room for a branded, quality-differentiated entrant to capture margin that currently accrues to intermediaries.

## 7. Investment Economics, Project Financials & Risk Profile

### Investment Economics, Project Financials & Risk Profile — Pakistan Solar Thermal

**Ground-Up CAPEX Picture**

Solar thermal investment in Pakistan spans a wide range of system types, each with materially different cost profiles and return characteristics.

At the smallest scale, **residential solar water heaters (SWHs)** — predominantly ETC systems — sell at PKR 115,000–360,000 for 150–300 litre units (approximately $400–$1,300 at ~PKR 280/USD) (Source: https://thesolarpanelprice.pk/solar-water-heater-price-in-pakistan-2024/). This translates to roughly $250–$650 per m² of collector area installed, broadly in line with South Asian regional benchmarks of $200–$500/m² for ETC systems (Source: https://sigmaearth.com/calculating-the-cost-of-solar-water-heating-systems-what-you-need-to-know/). Using ~0.7 kWth per m² of ETC collector, residential SWH CAPEX is roughly **$360–$930/kWth** in Pakistan.

**Large commercial/hotel-scale systems** (5–50 kWth) carry installed costs of approximately $500–$900/kWth in comparable South Asian markets, inclusive of tanks, pumps, controllers, and civil works. Pakistan-specific commercial system quotations were not found in public sources [UNVERIFIED for Pakistan large commercial].

**Industrial process heat (SHIP) systems** using flat-plate or ETC collectors at scale: international benchmarks suggest $400–$800/kWth for medium-sized deployments, with economies of scale kicking in above ~500 kWth.

**Concentrating solar thermal (parabolic trough)** for electricity or high-temperature industrial heat is a different order of magnitude. NREL's parabolic trough SHIP cost analysis puts solar field-only installed cost at $184–$297/m² depending on field size (Source: https://research-hub.nrel.gov/en/publications/parabolic-trough-collector-cost-update-for-industrial-process-hea/). For utility-scale CSP power generation, IRENA reports average installed cost of $3,677/kWe in 2024 (Source: https://www.irena.org/-/media/Files/IRENA/Agency/Publication/2025/Jul/IRENA_TEC_RPGC_in_2024_Summary_2025.pdf). No parabolic trough CSP projects are operational in Pakistan; this technology remains pre-commercial in the country.

**Operating Economics and Payback Periods**

The investment case rests on displacing one of two fuels: natural gas or grid electricity.

Pakistan's gas tariffs for industrial consumers: approximately Rs 2,150–2,350/MMBtu (~$7.7–$8.4/MMBtu) effective July 2025; RLNG-supplied consumers pay approximately $13.7–$14.1/MMBtu (Source: https://www.nation.com.pk/25-May-2024/ogra-raises-rlng-prices-by-up-to-6-5pc-for-sngpl-ssgc-consumers/). Converting: pipeline gas at Rs 2,350/MMBtu equates to roughly PKR 2.23/kWh thermal (~$0.008/kWh thermal). At RLNG rates of $14/MMBtu, the cost rises to ~$0.048/kWh thermal. Solar thermal competes much more favourably against RLNG than pipeline gas.

**Grid electricity** for commercial/industrial consumers: approximately Rs 31.59/kWh (~$0.113/kWh) base tariff in FY2025 (Source: https://profit.pakistantoday.com.pk/2024/06/21/nepra-introduces-new-fixed-charges-in-electricity-tariff-for-all-consumer-categories/). Solar thermal displacing electric water heating is the economically most compelling case — electricity being 10–15x more expensive per kWh thermal than pipeline gas.

Academic analysis of residential SWH in Islamabad found **payback periods of 1.16–1.38 years when displacing electricity**, and 6.95–8.27 years when displacing pipeline gas (Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959652617328391). For commercial/hotel systems displacing electricity, payback periods of 3–5 years are achievable. Egypt provides a useful comparable: a case study on industrial solar thermal found a payback period of **7.6 years, IRR of 14.3%, and ROI of 34.5% over 10 years** (Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11336367/).

**IRR Estimates**

No publicly available Pakistan-specific solar thermal financial models with IRR outputs were found. The closest proxies are: (1) Pakistan solar PV financial analyses put equity IRR at 9.6–14.9% for various Pakistani cities (Source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/energy-research/articles/10.3389/fenrg.2022.1028310/full); (2) the Egypt SHIP case study at 14.3% IRR; and (3) India solar project benchmarks where equity IRR has compressed to ~12%. For a Pakistan solar thermal ESCO or project developer targeting commercial/industrial segments and displacing high-cost electricity or RLNG, **equity IRR in the 12–18% range is plausible** under current tariff conditions, though this is a modelled inference rather than reported actuals. [UNVERIFIED — no published Pakistan solar thermal financial model found with verified IRR outputs]

**Financing Landscape**

The SBP's **Renewable Energy Financing Scheme** provides concessional debt at up to 6% p.a. for eligible projects up to Rs 6 billion, with tenors up to 12 years (Source: https://www.sbp.org.pk/Incen-others/Rene.asp). Total disbursements had reached Rs 94.7 billion by June 2024, though primarily oriented toward solar PV.

Commercial bank lending outside SBP schemes runs at KIBOR + spread, with KIBOR anchored to the SBP policy rate (11.5% as of April 2026, down from a peak of 22% in 2023) (Source: https://tradingeconomics.com/pakistan/interest-rate). Most solar thermal deployments today are **equity-only or supplier-financed**. ESCO models are gaining traction for solar PV — Yellow Door Energy, backed by a $65M IFC-led financing package, operates a lease-to-own model (Source: https://www.ifc.org/en/pressroom/2019/ifc-and-ifc-asset-management-company-invest-in-innovative-solar-power-company-in-mena/). No equivalent operator has been identified offering ESCO structures specifically for solar thermal in Pakistan.

**Pakistan-Specific Risk Factors**

Four risks materially affect investor returns:

**(1) Currency devaluation.** The PKR has depreciated approximately 65% against the USD between 2020 and 2024. CAPEX for imported equipment is USD-denominated; revenue streams are PKR. Solar thermal equipment (HS codes 8419.1990) currently carries 0% customs duty, but this is reversible without notice (Source: https://www.fbr.gov.pk/categ/customs-tariff/51149/70853/131188).

**(2) Gas price policy reversal — both directions.** Gas price hikes improve the solar thermal investment case. However, a subsidy reversal would erode savings. Pakistan's Rs 3.3 trillion gas sector circular debt represents systemic pressure toward continued price increases — beneficial for solar thermal economics but carrying political economy risk.

**(3) Policy and regulatory risk.** Net metering buyback rates were slashed by over 60% in 2024 for solar PV, demonstrating regulatory frameworks can be rewritten mid-cycle (Source: https://dialogue.earth/en/energy/opinion-pakistans-solar-journey-is-feeling-the-heat-from-cost-shifting/). Solar thermal, which does not feed into the grid, is less exposed to this specific risk.

**(4) Counterparty and institutional risk.** Pakistan's circular debt (PKR 5.42 trillion, with PKR 505 billion added in FY25) creates severe off-taker risk for grid-tied investments (Source: https://www.adb.org/sites/default/files/linked-documents/53165-002-ld-03.pdf). Solar thermal's key advantage: the off-taker is a **direct energy consumer** (hotel, hospital, factory) rather than a government utility — counterparty quality is driven by the creditworthiness of individual corporate buyers. This makes solar thermal meaningfully less exposed to circular debt dynamics than grid-connected solar PV.

**Business Model Assessment**

For a large capital deployer, three models are credible at scale:

- **ESCO/energy-as-a-service:** Build, own, and operate solar thermal assets on customer premises; charge a fixed monthly or per-kWh-thermal tariff below grid/gas cost. Recurring revenue, asset on investor's balance sheet, credit risk is C&I counterparty. Yellow Door Energy's IFC-backed model in solar PV is the closest precedent.
- **Portfolio EPC with holdco:** Develop projects across sectors (hotels, hospitals, dairy/food processing, textile mills displacing RLNG boilers), retain an equity stake in each SPV, and sell down to local investors. Recycles capital.
- **Equipment distribution + service:** Asset-light, but dependent on end-user financing access and not capital-efficient at scale for a large deployer.

The ESCO model is most capital-efficient for large deployment. The highest-conviction near-term segment is **hotels and hospitals displacing grid electricity**, where paybacks of 3–5 years are achievable and counterparty quality is manageable.

**Key Findings:**
- The most financially compelling deployment in Pakistan is solar thermal displacing grid electricity in commercial/hotel/hospital settings, with simple payback of 3–5 years — materially better than gas displacement (7–12 years given subsidised pipeline gas).
- No published Pakistan-specific solar thermal IRR models exist; Egypt SHIP comparable suggests 14.3% IRR at 7.6-year payback; Pakistan PV analogues suggest 12–15% equity IRR is achievable in favourable locations.
- SBP concessional debt at 6% p.a. is available in principle but practically oriented toward PV; most solar thermal projects today are equity-financed, representing a structural gap and an opportunity for a first-mover ESCO deployer.
- Currency devaluation (65% PKR decline since 2020) is the primary financial risk for USD-CAPEX/PKR-revenue models; however, solar thermal's direct-consumer off-taker structure avoids the circular debt counterparty risk that afflicts grid-connected solar PV investments.
