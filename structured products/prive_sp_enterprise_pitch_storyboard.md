# Privé Structured Products Engine — Enterprise Client Pitch Deck Storyboard

**Prepared:** 10 August 2026 (v2 — restructured around the agenda-led narrative) · **Scope:** Slide-by-slide storyboard for a single deck, pitched live to an enterprise client (a bank's buying committee). Not a final design. Companion to, and consistent with, the two-deck partner storyboard document.
**Conventions:** UK English. Headlines are full sentences carrying the argument (assertion–evidence). One idea per slide. All on-slide statistics carry a small-text source line. ⚠ marks a claim needing a proof point Privé may not yet hold — see the Proof-Point Gap Register at the end. Market, competitive and regulatory evidence draws on the internal research deep-dive *SP_research.pdf* (August 2026) plus the public sources listed in the References section. "NBA" (next-best-action) is used throughout for the recommendation engine — the same capability referred to as NBO (next-best-offer) in earlier internal documents.

**Narrative arc (the agenda, which is also the argument):** the SP market has grown into one of the biggest lines in the wealth book → but the industry runs it as a black box, and when clients lose money they blame the RM → the fix is lifecycle management plus a next-best-action engine → Privé delivers this as three concrete solutions (factsheets; lifecycle + NBA; product comparison and a transparent, risk-disclosed sales process) → shown on a real client example → small, reversible call to action.

## The Deck — Enterprise Client Pitch (presented to a bank's buying committee)

**Design brief in one line:** presented live by Privé to the people who would buy and the people who could veto — Head of Investment Products or SP desk head (the sponsor), plus compliance/second line, ops/IT and possibly a COO delegate. The sponsor buys revenue and control; the vetoes are suitability risk, integration risk and pricing credibility. Every slide either builds the sponsor's case or pre-empts a veto.

**Format notes:** 11 slides, ~40 minutes with demo and discussion. Presented, so headlines still carry the full argument (the deck must survive being re-read cold when the sponsor circulates it internally afterwards — an enterprise deck always has a second life as a forwarded PDF). Two moments are deliberately interactive: the book-size calculation on B6 and the client example on B10. Bank-specific fields marked [Bank] are populated from discovery before every meeting — presenting this deck ungrounded in their numbers wastes the room.

| B4 | **Pain point 1 — "After the trade, a structured product is a black box: no factsheet, no live valuation, no risk view — a term sheet PDF and a line in custody."** | The instrumentation gap is structural and industry-wide — funds get telemetry, SPs get silence. | The asymmetry in one table: what a fund gets (factsheet, NAV, performance history, benchmark, risk analytics, portfolio look-through) vs what an SP gets (term sheet PDF at issue, custody line, issuer mark — often stale — and nothing else). Why it persists: academic evidence that complexity is a feature of how these products are sold — retail structured products have grown measurably more complex over time, and complexity correlates with headline rates that obscure true risk *(Célérier & Vallée, "What Drives Financial Complexity?"; AMF complexity studies; SEC staff have publicly questioned whether retail investors understand what they hold)*. The operational consequence: RMs cannot answer "what is my note worth and what are my chances of losing money?" without calling the issuer — so mostly the question goes unanswered. | The ✓/✗ comparison table as the hero, funds column full of ticks, SP column nearly empty — the ✗ column does the arguing. Beneath, a small timeline of one note's life: a single document at t=0, then twelve months of blank space to maturity. | Establish the problem as structural, not a criticism of [Bank] — "every bank in this market has this gap." The blank-timeline visual is the emotional beat: let it sit for a moment. This slide sets up both pain point 2 (opacity is what converts a loss into a trust event) and the entire Privé section. |
| B5 | **Pain point 2 — "When an opaque product loses money, the client blames the RM: Korea's ELS episode turned HK$-index losses into US$3.2bn of compensation, branch bans and a trust crisis."** | Losses on products clients didn't understand destroy the *relationship*, not just the P&L — and nobody is managing the lifecycle that could pre-empt it. | The evidence chain: **(1) Losses become trust events when the product was a black box** — Korea's HSCEI-linked ELS crisis: ~KRW4.6tn (US$3.2bn) of retail losses in early 2024; regulators found customers had misinterpreted ELS as principal-guaranteed savings products; major banks set aside ~KRW1.7tn for compensation and ELS sales were restricted to 5–10% of ~3,900 branches *(Korea Herald / FSS; on-slide source line)*. **(2) HK is on the same fault line** — the SFC–HKMA April 2024 joint circular found PDD and suitability deficiencies with "particular focus on structured products", including frontline staff unable to explain accumulator mechanics and an FX-TARF classified "medium risk" despite unlimited-loss potential; a 2025 thematic review followed. **(3) And no one is proactively managing the lifecycle** — 73% of callable notes were called in 2024 (avg life ~12 months, US$39bn on one US platform alone *(iCapital 2024, US platform data, labelled)*); autocalls knock out in clustered waves *(FVC)* — yet most banks have no systematic alerting, so observation dates arrive unannounced and losses arrive unexplained. ⚠ Where available from discovery: [Bank]'s own complaint/attrition experience after SP losses. | A three-panel cause-and-effect strip: opaque product (black box icon) → loss event (index drawdown chart) → broken trust (client-departure arrow + the Korea headline as a pull-quote with the US$3.2bn figure). Beneath, a thin calendar strip dense with unwatched observation dates. | The deck's emotional core. Tell the Korea story in ninety seconds as a *systems* failure — product opacity + no lifecycle management + suitability gaps — not as "bad banks". Then bring it home: the HK regulator has already published the same failure modes. The unspoken message to the sponsor: your RMs carry this risk personally, every observation date. Watch compliance here — this slide recruits them. |
| B6 | **The fix — "A lifecycle tool watches every date in the book; a next-best-action engine turns each event into a suitable, explainable recommendation."** | The category answer (before the Privé pitch): managed lifecycle + governed reinvestment is what closes both pain points — and the money in motion makes it self-funding. | Definition of the fix, vendor-neutrally: **lifecycle management** — every KI/KO/autocall/maturity observation tracked with probabilities, alerts ahead of the event, RM briefed before the client calls; **next-best-action (NBA)** — at each event, candidate reinvestments generated from the client's holdings and profile, suitability-gated, with reasoning and audit trail. The book-size arithmetic, done live: [Bank]'s callable book [US$Xbn] × ~0.7 called p.a. *(iCapital 2024 proxy, US platform, labelled)* ≈ **[US$0.7Xbn] in motion every year** — every observation date is either recycled revenue or assets walking out the door. Regulatory tailwind, one line: explainable, auditable recommendation engines are where HK is pointing (HKMA GenAI guidance Aug 2024; HKMA/SFC GenA.I. Sandbox++ extended to wealth, March 2026) — worded as alignment, never endorsement. | The flow diagram, personalised: "[Bank]'s SP book" → calendar strip of observation dates → the fork: thick accent arrow "reinvested with [Bank]" vs grey arrow "idle cash / competitor's trade" leaking off-slide. ### Meeting mechanics notes

The deck assumes a ~40-minute slot: B1–B3 in eight minutes (agenda and the market story), B4–B6 in twelve (the two pain points and the fix — including the book-size moment on B6), B7–B10 in fifteen (the three solutions and the client example, demo included), B11 in five (the close). If the meeting is sponsor-only with no compliance present, keep B9's transparent-sales-process content anyway — the sponsor will be asked about it internally, and the Korea framing on B5 makes them want it. The PDF's second life matters: the sponsor will circulate it to exactly the people B8–B9 serve, so those slides must survive unpresented. Discovery is a precondition, not a nicety — B3's revenue share and B6's book size are [Bank] fields; presenting placeholders tells the room they are a segment, not a client. Integration and credibility material (architecture detail, reference deployments, team) now lives in the appendix rather than the main flow — bring it to the room, present it only if IT or procurement asks, and always attach it to the circulated PDF.

**Positioning discipline check:** execution rails are named and reassured, never disparaged (B11); the NBA suitability gate is presented as a control the bank acquires, not a vendor disclaimer (B8, B10), backed by the SEC anti-churn precedent; loss scenarios carry equal visual weight to coupons wherever products are compared (B9) — the transparency claim must be visibly true on Privé's own slides; the land-and-expand sequence is explicit (B11 pilot → lifecycle/NBA → comparison/solver); and no slide claims execution, RFQ or issuer-facing capability. The Korea episode (B5) is always told as a systems failure, never as an attack on named institutions.
